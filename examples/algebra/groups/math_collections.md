# Mathematical uses of shared group theory

Integer addition supplies a group whose multiplication, identity and inverse
are addition, zero and negation. The doubling map preserves addition, and its
only zero preimage is zero. The imported trivial-kernel theorem proves its
injectivity; the kernel construction then supplies a normal subgroup.

The one-element group is a legitimate instance. Its complete operation and
inverse maps have a singleton domain and codomain. Explicit finite enumeration
checks the laws, so excluding this case would be an accidental extra axiom.

The real Heisenberg group uses ordinary length-three real vectors as its
carrier. Associativity follows by expanding the third coordinate and
distributing multiplication over addition. Its inverse formula cancels that
coordinate. The central-coordinate function distinguishes the two generator
products and transports a hypothetical product equality to the contradiction
one equals zero. This verifies an actual noncommutative instance of the same
Group interface used by the integer example.

The rejection controls isolate theorem premises and group laws. Translation
does not preserve the additive identity; a zero homomorphism is not injective;
group laws do not imply commutativity; inversion cannot be replaced with the
zero map. These are boundaries of the shared definitions and theorem uses,
not failures to discover a missing proof of a true conclusion.
